All-weather floor mats for a Model 3 or Model Y — what to check before you order

People buy floor liners for a Tesla for a boring reason: slush, salt, wet shoes, and dog hair. If you live around Cincinnati or Northern Kentucky, that is a winter problem, not a style problem.

This is a buying checklist and a short comparison of the kinds of mats you will see, not a hands-on lab test of every SKU. I am not going to invent a price or tell you a mat “fits all years.” Tesla has changed the floor shape across Model 3 and Model Y refreshes. Look up your exact model, year, and body style on the product page.

Who this is for

  • You drive a Model 3 or Model Y and want the cabin to survive winter and car-pool mud.
  • You would rather spend once on a liner that catches water than keep vacuuming a soaked carpet.

Who this is not for

  • You want a plush carpet look and rarely carry wet gear.
  • You are shopping for a Cybertruck, Model S, or Model X — those are different floors. Do not use a 3/Y kit.

What actually matters (check these on the listing)

  1. Exact fitment. The listing should name Model 3 or Model Y and a year range. If it says “Tesla” only, skip it. If you have a Highland Model 3 or a Juniper Model Y (or any refresh), confirm that generation is listed. When in doubt, email the seller with your year and VIN-derived model before you buy.
  2. Coverage, not just “all weather.” Fronts only vs. a full set (front, rear, trunk or cargo). A front-only kit leaves the rear and the footwells that kids actually ruin.
  3. A raised edge and a channel. The point of a liner is to hold slush, not just sit on the carpet. Look for photos of a tall lip around the border.
  4. Material you can rinse. Most good options are a thermoplastic or rubber-style liner you can pull out and hose off. If the listing is vague, assume you will not know how it ages until you own it.
  5. Return policy and shipping time. A slightly more expensive kit you can return for a misfit is safer than a no-return import with a long ship time. Read that policy; do not trust the ad headline.

The main options, with honest tradeoffs

WeatherTech-style custom liners

WeatherTech is the name a lot of people already know for laser-measured liners. The usual pitch is a vehicle-specific mold and a high lip.

  • Pros: Specific year/model pages; easy to verify fitment if you use their vehicle selector; a long-standing return/warranty reputation you can read on their site (confirm current terms); works with a published affiliate program if you buy through a tracking link.
  • Cons: Often costs more than a generic kit. Some owners still argue about look and odor on first unboxing — that is a personal taste issue, not something I will pretend to have measured. You still have to pick the right year.

Tesla-specialty brands (TESMAG, Tesery, TAPTES, Hansshow, and similar)

These shops sell 3D-style TPE liners aimed at Tesla owners, often as a full set including a trunk or frunk piece.

  • Pros: Often sold as a more complete kit (cabin + cargo) than a fronts-only liner. 30-day cookies on several of their affiliate programs, which is more forgiving than Amazon’s short cookie if a reader thinks it over.
  • Cons: Quality varies by brand and by batch. Photos can be optimistic. Shipping time and fitment for a refresh model are the two things I would verify in writing. I will not rank these as “better than WeatherTech” or the reverse without a side-by-side in the same car.

Amazon generic / house-brand liners

Amazon has many “for Tesla Model Y” kits at a wide range of prices.

  • Pros: Fast shipping if the item is fulfilled locally; easy returns on many (not all) listings; useful if you want a cheap first set for a beater-winter and do not care about the brand.
  • Cons: The catalog is noisy. Two listings can look identical and fit different years. Amazon’s affiliate cookie is short (24 hours, plus their cart rule). Read recent reviews that mention your year, not only five-star summaries.

A straight recommendation

If you want the least drama, start with a vehicle-specific liner from a seller that shows your exact year and has a return path you have actually read. WeatherTech is the conservative version of that sentence. A Tesla-specialty full kit can make sense if the listing clearly includes your refresh year and cargo coverage you actually need. A no-name Amazon kit is fine if you accept that you may be the quality-control department.

I am not picking a single SKU here on purpose. The wrong SKU is worse than no recommendation.

Installation and care (the unglamorous part)

  • Let the car dry or at least shake out the factory carpet before the liner goes in, or you will trap grit.
  • After road salt, pull the liners and rinse them. Salt under a liner will still sit on the carpet.
  • If a liner does not sit flat, stop and re-check fitment. Forcing it usually means it is the wrong year.
