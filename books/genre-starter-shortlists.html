Books

Starter shortlists by genre: fiction, history, and useful nonfiction

Year-end “best of” lists are useful, but a lot of people do not want 2026 at all. They want a starting point in a genre they already like, or one they have been meaning to try.

This page is a starter map, not a canon, and not a claim that these are the only books worth reading. I have stuck to well-known, widely available titles so you can actually find a copy. If you already have a favorite in a category, skip my pick and stay with yours.

Fiction

If you want a novel that people have pressed into each other’s hands for years

  • To Kill a Mockingbird — Harper Lee. Still a common starting point for American fiction; also assigned so often that some readers would rather receive something else. Gift it only if you know they have not read it. Bookshop: Bookshop.org · Amazon: Amazon
  • Gilead — Marilynne Robinson. Quiet, serious, and religious in a way that is actually thought through. A better gift for a reflective reader than for someone who asked for a thriller. Bookshop: Bookshop.org · Amazon: Amazon
  • The Thursday Murder Club — Richard Osman. A lighter mystery, easy to wrap, not a literary test. Bookshop: Bookshop.org · Amazon: Amazon

If you want something newer

History

These are reported or narrative history books, not textbooks. They are also not a substitute for walking a place yourself.

  • 1776 — David McCullough. A readable account of a single year of the American Revolution. A solid gift for a reader who likes history without academic apparatus. Bookshop: Bookshop.org · Amazon: Amazon
  • The Splendid and the Vile — Erik Larson. Churchill’s household and London during the Blitz, told as narrative history. For readers who liked Larson’s other books, or who want WWII without a general’s-eye campaign survey. Bookshop: Bookshop.org · Amazon: Amazon
  • Battle Cry of Freedom — James M. McPherson. A one-volume Civil War history that is still widely recommended as a starting point. It is a long book. Do not gift it as a casual paperback unless you know the reader wants that depth. Bookshop: Bookshop.org · Amazon: Amazon
  • Say Nothing — Patrick Radden Keefe. Modern reporting on the Troubles in Northern Ireland. Gripping, and not gentle. Bookshop: Bookshop.org · Amazon: Amazon

Useful nonfiction (practical, not preachy)

I am deliberately skipping the usual “one habit will change your life” pile-on, because those books are already everywhere and often over-promise.

  • The Checklist Manifesto — Atul Gawande. A short book about why experts still use checklists, written by a surgeon. Useful if you like practical ideas without a guru tone. Bookshop: Bookshop.org · Amazon: Amazon
  • A Guide to the Good Life — William B. Irvine. A modern introduction to Stoic practice. For readers who want the philosophy in plain English. It is not a religious book, and it is not a substitute for one. Bookshop: Bookshop.org · Amazon: Amazon
  • On Writing — Stephen King. Half memoir, half craft. Interesting even if you never plan to write a novel, and especially if you do. Bookshop: Bookshop.org · Amazon: Amazon

How I would actually use this page

Tell me (or tell yourself) the genre and the reader’s tolerance for length and darkness. Then pick one book, not five. A gift list that ships a single title someone will finish is better than a stack they will not.

If a title here is one Max has not read and is not comfortable recommending under his name, it should be removed or relabeled as “a common starting point” before this page goes live. That edit is expected.
