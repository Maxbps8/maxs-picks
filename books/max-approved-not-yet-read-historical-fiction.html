Historical fiction that respects history

This is a Max Approved but Not Yet Read list: books I have chosen for my own to-read shelf, not books I have already finished. A later series, simply called Max Approved, will be reserved for books I have read and can recommend from experience.

I have not read the novels below. They are on this list because these writers are known for staying close to the historical record, and for treating great leaders and the people who served with them with respect. After I read them, I will come back with real reviews. Until then, I am only describing each book as it stands — the setting, the scope, and honors that can be checked.

Jeff Shaara — start with Rise to Rebellion or Gods and Generals

Jeff Shaara writes American war history as fiction, following real commanders and the people around them closely enough that the novels are often used as a readable companion to the record. He has written books on the American Revolution, the Civil War, and the Second World War. I have not read them yet. Two clear starting points are below.

Rise to Rebellion is a novel of the American Revolution, beginning with the Boston Massacre in 1770 and running through the decisions and battles that led to independence. It is told through figures such as John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, George Washington, and the British commander Thomas Gage, and it recreates events including the Boston Tea Party and the fighting at Concord and Bunker Hill. The sequel, The Glorious Cause, continues the war.

Rise to Rebellion, Ballantine paperback, on Amazon

Gods and Generals is Shaara’s novel of the Civil War’s early years, from the late 1850s through Chancellorsville in 1863. It is a prequel to his father Michael Shaara’s The Killer Angels, and it follows Robert E. Lee, Thomas “Stonewall” Jackson, Winfield Scott Hancock, and Joshua Lawrence Chamberlain. If you want the Civil War in this family of books, this is the earlier chapter; The Killer Angels is next on this list.

Michael Shaara — The Killer Angels

The Killer Angels is a novel of the Battle of Gettysburg, told from several commands on both sides of the field over the first three days of July 1863. Michael Shaara published it in 1974. It won the 1975 Pulitzer Prize for Fiction, and it later became the basis for the film Gettysburg. It remains the best-known Civil War novel in this line of books, and it is the middle volume in the story that Jeff Shaara later framed with Gods and Generals and The Last Full Measure.

The Killer Angels, Ballantine paperback, on Amazon

Herman Wouk — The Winds of War and War and Remembrance

These two novels follow one American family, especially U.S. Navy officer Victor “Pug” Henry, through the Second World War. The Winds of War, published in 1971, runs from the late 1930s through the attack on Pearl Harbor. War and Remembrance, published in 1978, continues the story through the end of the war, including the war in Europe, the Pacific, and the Holocaust. Together they are long, widely read novels of that war, later adapted as a television miniseries. Read The Winds of War first; the second book is a sequel, not a standalone.

Steven Pressfield — Gates of Fire

Gates of Fire is a novel of the Battle of Thermopylae in 480 B.C., when a small Spartan-led force held the pass against the Persian army of Xerxes. Pressfield published it in 1998. The story is told largely through a survivor’s account of the men who fought there, and it is widely read as a book about courage, discipline, and duty in the ranks. It has appeared on the Commandant’s Professional Reading List, including the 2019 list for entry-level Marines.

Gates of Fire, Bantam paperback, on Amazon

Conn Iggulden — the Conqueror series

Iggulden’s Conqueror novels are historical fiction about the rise of Genghis Khan and the Mongol Empire. The first book was published in Britain as Wolf of the Plains and in the United States as Genghis: Birth of an Empire. It follows Temujin from his youth on the steppe — exile, survival, and the early raids that begin to unite rival tribes — before the later volumes carry the story through the building of the empire. If you want the history alongside the novel, Jack Weatherford’s Genghis Khan and the Making of the Modern World is a clear nonfiction companion.

Genghis Khan and the Making of the Modern World, Crown paperback, on Amazon

Bernard Cornwell — Sharpe’s Tiger and The Last Kingdom

Cornwell writes action-heavy historical novels and, in each book, adds an author’s note that tells the reader what is drawn from the record and what he invented. That habit is one reason these books are on this to-read shelf.

Sharpe’s Tiger is the first story, in historical order, in the Richard Sharpe series. It is set in India in 1799, at the siege of Seringapatam, and it follows Sharpe as a private in the British army. The series later moves to the Napoleonic Wars.

The Last Kingdom opens Cornwell’s Saxon Stories (also published as the Last Kingdom series). It is set in ninth-century England, in the wars between the Saxon kingdoms and the Danes, and it follows the fictional Uhtred of Bebbanburg alongside the historical King Alfred. It later became the basis for the television series of the same name.
