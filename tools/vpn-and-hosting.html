A lot of VPN and hosting ads sound like the internet is on fire and you need to buy a subscription this afternoon. You usually do not.

This is a plain guide to two tools people actually ask about — a VPN for travel and public Wi-Fi, and a paid web host for a small site — and when you can skip both. I am not going to invent a price, a speed test, or a “best of 2026” ranking. Check live plans on the official pages.

Who this is for

  • You use cafe, hotel, or airport Wi-Fi and want a straightforward way to encrypt that connection.
  • You are thinking about a small website for a project or a local business, and you are not sure whether you need to pay for hosting.
  • You want the honest limits, not a scare story.

Who this is not for

  • You are trying to hide illegal activity. A VPN is not a disguise, and I will not help with that.
  • You want investment, tax, or “set up an online empire” advice. This is not that.
  • You only need a simple public page with no login, no email inbox, and no WordPress dashboard. A free host such as GitHub Pages is enough for that. This site is built to run that way.

What a VPN actually does

On public Wi-Fi, a VPN encrypts the traffic between your device and the VPN company’s server, so someone on the same network has a harder time reading that traffic. That is the useful, boring case: a hotel lobby, an airport gate, a coffee shop.

A VPN does not:

  • Make you invisible. The VPN company can still see that your account connected, and sites you log into still know you are you.
  • Replace good passwords, account recovery hygiene, or software updates.
  • Guarantee that a particular streaming library or regional site will work. Those rules change, and I will not promise them.
  • Fix a compromised email account or a leaked password.

If you rarely leave a trusted home network, you may not need a paid VPN at all. If you travel, or you work from public networks, it can be a reasonable precaution.

NordVPN, as a first paid option

NordVPN is a well-known consumer VPN with apps for common phones and computers. I am pointing you at the official site so you can read the current plans, device limits, refund window, and feature list for yourself.

What I would actually do if I were shopping:

  1. Open the official NordVPN site and, if you want the explainer in their words, their What is a VPN page.
  2. Read the current subscription options on their pricing page. I am not quoting a number here because those offers change.
  3. Read the refund or money-back terms in the same place you would pay. If that language is vague, wait.
  4. Install the app from the official site or the official app store for your phone, not from a random ad.

NordVPN is a fit if you want a paid VPN from a company that has a clear consumer product and a public refund policy, and you are willing to pay for it. It is not a requirement for owning a computer.

I am not claiming it is the fastest service, the cheapest, or the only responsible choice. I have not run a lab test for this article.

A reasonable alternative, if you want a comparison

Surfshark is another paid consumer VPN with a public site you can read the same way: plans, device policy, and refund terms, straight from the company. If you are comparing, compare the live terms, not a third-party “best VPN” list.

I am not picking a winner between them in this guide.

A simple web host, when you actually need one

Hosting is a different question from a VPN.

You do not need a paid host if:

  • You want a static public site (articles, a menu, a church bulletin, a family history page) and you are comfortable with a simple setup. GitHub Pages is free and is how this site is meant to be published.
  • You only need a one-page link list. Several free options exist; I would start with the one you already understand.

You might want a paid host if:

  • You need WordPress, a CMS login, or a plugin ecosystem.
  • You want email at your own domain (you@yourproject.com).
  • You need a site builder, staging, or a control panel, and you do not want to maintain a server yourself.

Hostinger

Hostinger sells web hosting and related services (including WordPress-oriented plans and domains) to people who want a conventional paid host. Start at their official site, then read a current web hosting plan page.

Same rules as the VPN:

  • I will not quote a monthly price. Look at the live offer and notice whether the renewal price differs from the first term. Many hosts advertise a low first invoice and a higher renewal.
  • Read the refund window and what happens if you cancel.
  • Do not buy a four-year plan for a project you have not started. You can usually begin with the shortest term that still includes the features you need, then renew if the site is actually in use.

Hostinger is a fit if you have a real reason to pay for hosting and you want a mainstream, documented product. It is not a fit if a free static site would do the same job.

Bluehost is a reasonable official alternative to read in the same way if you want a second quote. I am not ranking the two.

Shopify is not hosting, and it can wait

Shopify is a hosted store platform — a cart, checkout, and storefront — not a generic website host. If you do not have products, shipping, and a reason to take payment, you do not need it yet.

When a hobby actually needs a shopping cart, read the current plans on Shopify’s official site, including what happens when a trial ends. I will not pretend a trial is “free forever,” and I will not walk you through a store launch in this article.

A straight recommendation

  • For travel and public Wi-Fi, a paid VPN can be sensible. Start with the official NordVPN pages and read the live terms. Compare Surfshark if you want a second option. Do not buy one because an ad told you that your home internet is unsafe.
  • For a simple public website, try a free static host such as GitHub Pages first. Pay Hostinger (or another host you have actually compared) when you need WordPress, email, or a control panel that GitHub Pages does not provide.
  • Leave Shopify until you are truly selling something.

If a salesperson — or an affiliate article, including this one — is pushing you to bundle all three today, slow down. Buy the one tool you have a present use for, or buy none.
